Chapter Three

The ferry left at six, and by the time the fog lifted the harbour was only a grey line behind them. Mara kept her notebook on her knee and wrote down everything: the colour of the water, the gulls that followed the wake, the way the engine changed its note whenever the boat turned into the wind.

Her uncle had told her the island was smaller than it looked on the map. He had not said that the map was forty years old, or that half the roads on it no longer went anywhere at all.

When the pier came into view she counted the houses along the shore. There were eleven, and only three had smoke rising from their chimneys. On the hill above them stood the lighthouse, white and patient, its lamp dark in the morning light.

"You'll want to walk," said the ferryman, tying off the rope. "Nothing on wheels gets far here."

She thanked him and stepped onto the boards, which moved a little under her feet, as if the pier itself were still deciding whether to let her stay.
